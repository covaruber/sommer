Factor analytic and reduced rank models in sommer

Giovanny Covarrubias-Pazaran

2026-10-19

The sommer package was developed to provide R users with a flexible univariate and multivariate linear mixed-model solver. Multi-environment trial (MET) analyses often need a genetic covariance structure among environments. A fully unstructured covariance (usm()) captures every pairwise environment relationship but requires \(q(q+1)/2-1\) parameters for \(q\) environments, which quickly becomes difficult to estimate reliably as the number of environments grows. Factor-analytic (FA) and reduced-rank (RR) models approximate that same covariance with far fewer parameters by assuming that genotype-by-environment interaction is driven by a small number of latent factors.

This vignette focuses on the fam() and rrm() covariance-shaping factors used inside vsm(), and on the loadings_mmes()/scores_mmes() helper functions used to extract and visualize their fitted quantities.

SECTION 1: Theory

1) Why reduce the cross-environment covariance

Let \(q\) be the number of environments and \(G\) the \(q\times q\) genetic covariance matrix among them. An unstructured model estimates every variance and covariance directly, \(q(q+1)/2-1\) working parameters after removing the single overall scale owned by vsm(). As \(q\) grows, this saturated model becomes weakly identified relative to the available genotype replication, and its estimates become unstable. Diagonal (dsm()) and compound-symmetry (csm()) models are far more parsimonious but assume, respectively, no genetic correlation among environments or one common correlation everywhere. Factor-analytic and reduced-rank models sit between these extremes: they estimate genuine environment-specific covariance patterns while controlling the number of parameters through a rank \(k \ll q\).

2) The factor-analytic (FA) model

The FA model represents the environment covariance shape as

$$ K = \Lambda\Lambda^{\mathsf T} + \Psi, $$

where \(\Lambda\) is a \(q\times k\) loading matrix and \(\Psi\) is a diagonal matrix of environment-specific variances. Each environment’s genetic variance is split into a part explained by the \(k\) common latent factors (\(\Lambda\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\)) and a part unique to that environment (\(\Psi\)). vsm() still owns the single overall variance scale, so fam(x, k) reports a normalized shape with \(K_{11}=1\); the number of estimated working parameters for \(k\) factors and \(q\) environments is

$$ kq-\frac{k(k-1)}{2} + (q-1), $$

the first term for the (rotationally-constrained, lower-triangular) loadings and the second for the environment-specific variance ratios.

3) The reduced-rank (RR) model

The RR model uses the same loading structure but fixes every specific variance to zero:

$$ K = \Lambda\Lambda^{\mathsf T},\qquad \Lambda_{11}=1. $$

rrm(x, k) therefore has

$$ kq-\frac{k(k-1)}{2}-1 $$

free shape parameters, plus the overall scale supplied by vsm(): exactly \(q\) fewer parameters than heterogeneous fam(x, k). The covariance has rank \(k\), with no diagonal remainder, matching ASReml’s default rr() covariance family. The Henderson engine works with latent factors rather than inverting the singular environment covariance; direct inversion is also supported.

For an estimated common nonzero specific variance, use fam(x, k, variance="homogeneous"). This is the covariance family previously fitted by rrm(); the default fam() remains heterogeneous.

4) Choosing k and comparing to other structures

Increasing \(k\) enlarges the latent subspace, but RR remains singular while \(k<q\). In practice \(k\) is chosen small enough to remain identifiable and interpretable (often 1-3 factors). Zero-specific RR lies on the boundary of the FA family, so ordinary chi-squared likelihood-ratio reference distributions need not apply.

scores_mmes() predicts each genotype’s position on the \(k\) latent factors from its BLUPs, the fitted loadings, and the fitted covariance. We use the default regression method and varianceScale = TRUE: scores are then on the standardized latent-factor scale, with the overall genetic scale absorbed into the corresponding loadings. The term “regression” here describes the score estimator, not a new regression fitted to the plotted points.

3) Why and how to rotate loadings and scores

The fitted covariance identifies a latent subspace, not a unique set of factor axes. For any orthogonal \(k\times k\) matrix \(Q\), with \(Q Q^{\mathsf T}=I\),

$$ (\Lambda Q)(\Lambda Q)^{\mathsf T}=\Lambda\Lambda^{\mathsf T}. $$

Consequently, many loading matrices describe exactly the same model. The lower-triangular constraints used during fitting identify one parameterization, but its axes depend on the ordering of environments and need not be convenient for interpretation. Rotation at extraction is a change of coordinates, not a refit: it leaves the likelihood, specific variances, genetic covariance, and fitted genotype-by-environment effects unchanged.

Both loadings_mmes() and scores_mmes() accept the logical argument rotation:

  • rotation = FALSE retains the fitted, lower-triangular loading orientation.
  • rotation = TRUE (the default) rotates using the right singular vectors of the loading matrix. If \(\Lambda=A D V^{\mathsf T}\) is its singular value decomposition, sommer uses \(Q=-V\) and returns \(\Lambda_* = \Lambda Q\). The loading columns are then orthogonal, ordered by decreasing squared length. This is an SVD rotation, not a varimax or oblique rotation; no rotation name or custom matrix is accepted by this argument.

The rotated first factor captures the largest share of the common genetic variance; subsequent factors capture decreasing shares. When first-factor loadings have the same sign across environments, that factor can describe broad performance, while later factors describe contrasting responses or crossover interaction. This interpretation is data-dependent: the first factor is not necessarily an overall-performance axis, and later factors are not automatically particular environmental variables.

Always rotate loadings and scores together. Writing \(L=\sqrt{\sigma^2}\Lambda\) for the loadings in trait units and \(F\) for the genotype-by-factor score matrix, the common genetic prediction is

$$ \widehat U_{\mathrm{common}} = F L^{\mathsf T}. $$

With \(L_* = LQ\) and \(F_* = FQ\), this product is unchanged. Mixing rotated scores with unrotated loadings gives incorrect reconstructions and misleading plots. Keep term, method, and varianceScale consistent across extraction calls as well. Here the normalized loadings in faInfo are converted to trait units explicitly to match faScores:

faUnrotated <- loadings_mmes(fitFA, varianceScale = FALSE,
                           rotation = FALSE)
scoresUnrotated <- scores_mmes(fitFA, method = "regression",
                              varianceScale = TRUE, rotation = FALSE)
L <- sqrt(faInfo$sigma2) * faInfo$loadings
LUnrotated <- sqrt(faUnrotated$sigma2) * faUnrotated$loadings
common <- faScores %*% t(L)
commonUnrotated <- scoresUnrotated %*% t(LUnrotated)

stopifnot(
  isTRUE(all.equal(tcrossprod(L), tcrossprod(LUnrotated))),
  isTRUE(all.equal(common, commonUnrotated)),
  isTRUE(all.equal(faInfo$specific, faUnrotated$specific))
)
round(crossprod(L), 3)

##        F1     F2
## F1 49.063  0.000
## F2  0.000 12.229

The same arguments and reasoning apply to fitRR. For one factor there is no multi-axis rotation, only a possible sign change. A factor’s sign is arbitrary: reversing both a loading column and its score column changes neither the covariance nor the common predictions. Thus a negative score is not intrinsically “poor performance”; interpret it together with the loadings. Across separate fits, axes can also swap or become unstable when singular values are nearly equal, so raw score signs and labels should not be compared without aligning the axes.

This extraction argument is distinct from vsm(..., Gu = Gu, rotation = TRUE), which requests Lee–van der Werf rotation of a relationship term during fitting, not the post-fit orientation of FA/RR factors.

4) Percentage of variance explained

A standard factor-analytic diagnostic is the proportion of total genetic variance attributed to each latent factor:

varPerFactor <- colSums(faInfo$loadings^2)
totalVar <- sum(faInfo$loadings^2) + sum(faInfo$specific)
propExplained <- varPerFactor / totalVar
round(100 * propExplained, 1)

##   F1   F2 
## 48.7 12.1

These percentages use the normalized loadings and specific variances, so the common overall scale cancels. Their sum is the percentage of total genetic variance explained by the common factors, not necessarily 100%. Rotation redistributes variance between factors but preserves that sum. Under the default SVD rotation, factors are ordered by decreasing contribution; the percentages refer to variance across environments, not phenotypic variance or genotype-specific prediction accuracy.

7) Latent regression plots

In the FA literature, a latent regression plot displays genotype performance against an environmental coordinate estimated by the model: a factor loading. Unlike regression on measured rainfall or temperature, the horizontal coordinate is latent and learned from the genetic covariance. Each genotype has its own regression slope, given by its factor score. Such plots help distinguish broad adaptation from sensitivity to the environmental contrasts captured by a factor; see Smith and Cullis (2018) for FA-based selection tools and interpretation of performance and stability.

For genotype \(g\) in environment \(e\), the genetic effect is decomposed as

$$ \widehat u_{ge} = \sum_{j=1}^k \widehat f_{gj} L_{ej} + \widehat\delta_{ge}, $$

where \(\widehat\delta_{ge}\) is the predicted environment-specific remainder. In a one-factor model the common response is the straight line \(\widehat f_{g1} L_{e1}\) through the origin. With multiple factors, plotting the full genetic effect against only one loading also includes contributions from the other factors. To isolate factor \(j\), subtract those contributions first:

$$ \widehat u_{ge}^{(j)} = \widehat u_{ge}

  • \sum_{h\ne j}\widehat f_{gh} L_{eh} = \widehat f_{gj} L_{ej} + \widehat\delta_{ge}. $$

The following base-R plots show these adjusted genetic BLUPs as points and the common-factor response as a line. We select four genotypes spanning the rotated first-factor scores to keep the display readable. Their selection is illustrative, not a ranking rule.

U <- fitFA$uList[[faInfo$term]]
U <- U[rownames(faScores), rownames(L), drop = FALSE]
specificBLUP <- U - common
scoreOrder <- order(faScores[, 1])
selected <- scoreOrder[unique(round(seq(1, length(scoreOrder),
                                       length.out = 4)))]
genotypeColors <- c("steelblue4", "tomato3", "darkgreen", "goldenrod3")

local({
  oldPar <- par(mfrow = c(1, ncol(L)), mar = c(4.5, 4.5, 3, 1))
  on.exit(par(oldPar))
  for (factorIndex in seq_len(ncol(L))) {
    loading <- L[, factorIndex]
    factorResponse <- outer(faScores[selected, factorIndex], loading)
    adjusted <- factorResponse + specificBLUP[selected, , drop = FALSE]
    plot(range(loading), range(c(adjusted, factorResponse, 0)),
         type = "n", xlab = paste("Environment loading:", colnames(L)[factorIndex]),
         ylab = "Adjusted genetic effect", main = paste("Latent regression", factorIndex))
    abline(h = 0, v = 0, lty = 3, col = "grey70")
    for (genotypeIndex in seq_along(selected)) {
      points(loading, adjusted[genotypeIndex, ], pch = 16,
             col = genotypeColors[genotypeIndex])
      abline(a = 0, b = faScores[selected[genotypeIndex], factorIndex],
             col = genotypeColors[genotypeIndex], lwd = 2)
    }
    legend("topleft", legend = rownames(faScores)[selected],
           col = genotypeColors[seq_along(selected)], pch = 16, lty = 1,
           cex = 0.7, bty = "n")
  }
})

Each point represents an environment, located at its loading on that factor. A steep slope indicates a strong response to that latent environmental contrast; a nearly flat slope indicates little response along that axis. Opposite slopes indicate contrasting responses. Points away from a genotype’s line reflect the environment-specific remainder after removing the other factors, not raw observation residuals. The lines use model-derived scores; they are not ordinary least-squares regressions fitted to these points.

These are plots of genetic deviations, not absolute yield: the fixed environment effects are omitted, so all common-factor lines pass through zero and their crossings alone do not establish differences in total yield. For selection, consider the full predicted genetic effects (or predictions including fixed effects), all retained factors, specific effects, and prediction uncertainty. The points are shrunken BLUPs, and uncertainty in both loadings and scores is not shown. The default regression scores and Bartlett scores need not give the same slopes; use one method consistently. To draw analogous RR plots, extract the RR loadings and scores with the same rotation and scale choices and use the RR term’s BLUP matrix.

8) Heatmap of the fitted genetic correlation matrix

The normalized loadings and specific variances returned by loadings_mmes() can be combined directly into the fitted genetic covariance, and then converted to a correlation matrix for visualization.

Sigma <- faInfo$sigma2 * (
  faInfo$loadings %*% t(faInfo$loadings) + diag(faInfo$specific)
)
corMat <- cov2cor(Sigma)
stopifnot(isTRUE(all.equal(unname(Sigma),
                          unname(fitFA$theta[[faInfo$term]]))))
heatmap(corMat, symm = TRUE,
        main = "Fitted genetic correlation among environments")

Environments with a strong positive fitted correlation cluster together in the heatmap, while environments explained by different latent factors, or with a large specific variance, appear less correlated with the rest.
